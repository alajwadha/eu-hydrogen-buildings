# Stance: what the author is allowed to claim, and how firmly

The other references cover how a sentence is built. This one covers the position the
author takes behind it. Six things, each of which a draft written in a working-paper or
policy-report voice gets wrong in a way no grammar check will catch.

## Contents

1. Delivering an unfavourable result
2. Policy implications, and why the corpus never recommends
3. The hedging ladder
4. Register at the conclusion
5. The limitations block
6. Disagreeing with a published number

## 1. Delivering an unfavourable result

**The finding.** Across all fourteen papers I can find eight sentences that state a
result in negative grammar. `not viable` appears 0 times, `least promising` 0,
`economically unattractive` 0, `the worst` once. This literature almost never says that a
technology fails.

That is not because nothing fails in it. Things fail constantly. They are worded
differently.

**Rule: an unfavourable result is delivered as a comparison, in positive grammar, with the
losing option as the subject and a named cause attached.**

The corpus's own instances:

> The soluble method is more economical compared to the drilling method.

> ... is less favourable compared to L1.

> S4 achieves an energy efficiency of 52.70 %, **which is lower than** the 88–89 %
> efficiency of ABS [79] **but addresses the limitation of** ABS in storage duration.

> The heat storage proportion of the MS system **is only** 5.6 %, **which is much lower
> than that of** the 63.7 % of the MS-SA system, **indicating that** the heat storage
> ability of the conventional system is insufficient.

> **Neither pathway** of H2P nor A2P **achieves lifecycle profitability.**

The last one is the strongest negative statement in the corpus and it is worth studying,
because it shows what is permitted. It is short. It names the two pathways. It uses a
concrete financial criterion (*lifecycle profitability*) rather than a verdict word. There
is no adverb of disappointment, no *unfortunately*, no *fails to*, no *cannot*. And it sits
in the abstract, not buried.

**The conversion.** A draft that says

> Hydrogen is not a viable option for building heat in most of the region.

should say

> Hydrogen delivers heat at a higher cost than the heat pump in *N* of *M* regions, because
> the conversion chain loses *X* per cent of the input before the boiler and the boiler
> then delivers at a coefficient of performance of one.

Same claim. The second version names the population, gives the mechanism, and lets the
reader draw the verdict. That is how this journal loses an argument on purpose.

**Three specific words to remove**, since each carries a verdict the corpus never issues in
its own voice: *viable* as a bare adjective, *fails*, *rules out*. The corpus does use
*economically viable* and *favorable economic viability* but always attached to a stated
threshold, e.g. *below $1.02/kg, indicating favorable economic viability*.

**The word `only`.** It appears 153 times and is the corpus's main intensifier for a
disappointing quantity. *is only 5.6 %*. It survives verbatim into the conclusion. If a
number is meant to read as small, *only* is the sanctioned way to say so, and it is the
whole budget. Do not add *merely*, *just*, *barely* or *a mere*.

## 2. Policy implications, and why the corpus never recommends

**`we recommend` appears 0 times.** `it is recommended` appears twice. In fourteen papers
whose stated purpose is to inform decisions, nobody recommends anything.

What they do instead is grant the reader a capability. The construction is a result clause
followed by an enabling verb with the decision maker as its object:

> By disaggregating energy consumption at the sectoral, end-use, and regional levels,
> **policymakers can identify** critical areas for intervention.

> ... **enabling policymakers to evaluate** the trade-offs between ...

> ... **provide decision-makers with** probability-weighted deployment ranges.

> The results **will assist policymakers in** formulating ...

> ... **support decision-makers in** designing targeted interventions that align with ...

The verbs are *enable*, *assist*, *support*, *provide*, *allow*, *inform*. The subject of
the action is always the policymaker, never the author. The paper is the instrument and the
reader is the agent.

**The conversion.** A draft that says

> Governments should therefore prioritise heat pump deployment over hydrogen for buildings
> and redirect the associated subsidies.

should say

> The regional cost ordering reported here allows a national programme to identify where
> heat pump deployment is already the cheaper option at current capital costs, and where
> the ordering is sensitive to the electricity to gas price ratio.

The recommendation is still legible. It is just not stated by the authors, which is what
keeps a referee from writing *the policy conclusions overreach the analysis*.

**The one licensed exception** is `should be`, 53 uses, but read where they fall: almost
all are methodological rather than political, of the form *the results should be
interpreted as reflecting changes in site selection rather than technology cost*, or *future
work should extend the model to multi-well configurations*. `should` instructs the reader
how to read the paper, or instructs the next researcher. It does not instruct a government.

**Vocabulary that is welcome**: `incentiv-` 36, `subsid-` 19, `regulator` 36. Naming the
instrument is fine. Telling someone to pull it is not.

## 3. The hedging ladder

`wording.md` covers reporting verbs, which set how strongly the evidence speaks. This is the
separate question of how strongly the author speaks.

| Modal | Uses | What it licenses |
|---|---|---|
| `can` | 362 | A capability the model demonstrates. The default. |
| `could` | 91 | A projection or a scenario outcome, not yet demonstrated |
| `will` | 70 | Near-certainty, usually about a policy already legislated or an arithmetic consequence |
| `may` | 66 | A possibility the authors have not tested |
| `should` | 63 | Methodological instruction, or advice to future work |
| `would` | 60 | The counterfactual arm of a comparison |
| `must` | 23 | A physical or accounting constraint, rarely an obligation on a person |
| `might` | 8 | Effectively unused |

**Epistemic adverbs are absent.** `arguably` 0, `appears to` 0, `presumably` 1, `seems to`
1. Confidence in this journal is carried entirely by the modal verb and by the reporting
verb. There is no adverb layer.

This matters because a nervous author reaches for the adverb layer, and every one of those
reaches marks the draft as foreign to the venue. *This arguably suggests that costs may
possibly be somewhat higher* is four hedges and zero information. The corpus writes *costs
are 12 % higher under the high-price assumption*, or if genuinely uncertain, *costs could
reach ...*.

**Ranking of what to write when uncertain**, strongest to weakest, all attested:

1. `X is 12 % higher than Y`, when the model says so and no hedge is needed
2. `X can reach 12 %`, attainable under conditions stated in the same sentence
3. `X could reach 12 %`, a scenario projection
4. `X may reach 12 %`, plausible and untested
5. Do not go further. There is no fifth rung in this journal.

`likely` (29) and `unlikely` (4) are permitted as adjectives about outcomes. `probably`,
`conceivably`, `to some extent` are not in the corpus at all.

## 4. Register at the conclusion

Conclusions run **470 to 620 words**. Thirteen of the fourteen contain **no first person at
all**. The subject is `this study`, `this work`, `the results`, or the numbered finding
itself.

This is a genuine register shift. Bodies do use *we*, though sparingly, and *We* opens only
22 of 4,788 body sentences. At the conclusion the first person disappears completely.

The house frames, all attested:

> This study proposes ...
> This work bridges a relevant gap in the literature by providing ...
> This work lies in the development of a reproducible framework for ...
> The main conclusions of this work can be summarised as follows.
> This study is the first of its kind to ...

**Conclusions are frequently numbered.** The dominant layout is a short framing paragraph,
then `(1)` through `(4)` or `(5)`, one finding each, each carrying its number:

> (2) The heat storage proportion and overall energy efficiency of the molten salt system
> are only 5.6 % and 7.1 %, while the molten salt coupled steam accumulator system can
> achieve 63.7 % and 63.5 %, respectively.

> (3) DGR are most suitable for low-frequency operation. For SC, the LCOHS decreases from
> $0.70/kg to $0.38/kg as the annual frequency increases from 1 to 5 cycles.

Each numbered item is self-contained and carries its own figures. A reader who reads only
the conclusion gets every headline number. That is the design intent, and it is why the
numbers repeat from the abstract rather than being referred back to.

**Then future work, also numbered**, using the same bracketed scheme, e.g.
*(4) Expanded techno-economic assessment — Future evaluations could incorporate ...*.

## 5. The limitations block

`limitation` appears 38 times, `caveat` 0, `we acknowledge` once. Limitations are stated,
and stated impersonally.

**The structure is: announce, enumerate, then reverse.**

> This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged.

> ... First, ... Second, ... Third, future work should ...

> **Despite these limitations, the adopted assumptions are necessary to** ...

That closing concession reversal is the load-bearing sentence, and a draft that lists
limitations without it reads as an invitation to reject.

**The best single sentence in the corpus for our purposes** shows the full move inside one
sentence: name the assumption, state the *direction* of the bias it introduces, then state
what it buys:

> This discrepancy is primarily due to the assumption of horizontal panel orientation
> without tilt-angle optimization, **which is expected to underestimate PV capacity factors
> relative to optimally tilted systems but avoids overestimating performance given the
> uncertain impact** of wave-induced motions on OFPV systems [34].

Underestimate, not merely "differ". Naming the sign of the error is what converts an
admission into a defence, because a referee can then check that the bias runs against the
paper's own conclusion rather than for it. Any simplification whose bias runs *towards* the
paper's headline needs this sentence and needs it early.

**What the corpus never does**: apologise, call a limitation *significant*, promise to fix
it in a later paper by name, or bury it in a footnote. Also absent is `does not capture`
(0 uses); the attested forms are `is not considered` (2), `were excluded` (4), `beyond the
scope` (5).

**Assumptions get their justification in the same sentence that makes them**, which is the
prophylactic version of a limitation:

> To simplify the modeling process, it is assumed here that SOFC and DA-SOFC ...
> For the present study, the qf is set to 0.8 **as a conservative estimation** for
> array-interaction losses, **consistent with the reported values** for WEC arrays in the
> literature [51,52], and is assumed constant across all locations.

Note the two-part warrant: *conservative* names the direction, *consistent with [51,52]*
names the external support. Both in one clause.

## 6. Disagreeing with a published number

This is the thinnest area in the corpus, and the thinness is itself the finding.

**Comparison in this literature is overwhelmingly internal.** The `higher than that of`
family runs to roughly two dozen uses and almost all of them compare the paper's own cases
with each other. When the corpus does reach outside, it reaches for agreement, not
conflict, and it does so mostly to justify an *input* rather than to validate an *output*:

> A social discount factor of 8% is assumed **in agreement with previous PtG studies**
> [19,20,61].
> Adding PSA CAPEX, this assumption **is consistent with that in** [31].
> For land storage, we assumed 0.1%/d based on the KHI design, **consistent with
> observations from** the Linde's large-scale tank [29].
> ... **consistent with the findings of** a recent study [65].

**There is exactly one clean instance of contradicting a published number**, and it is the
template:

> **Musa et al. report a lower LCOH of USD$1.99/kg, a difference primarily attributable to
> the substantial economies of scale in their larger model.**

One sentence. Author name, their number, and the mechanism that accounts for the gap. No
adjective on their work, no claim that they were wrong, no defence of the present paper. The
difference is explained, not adjudicated.

One further instance, disagreeing with a model rather than a number, is equally flat:

> Conversely, the findings of Yang and Williams [33] (marked in yellow) **do not fully
> corroborate** the efficacy of the model in forecasting market trends.

**The general grammar for explaining any gap**, used throughout for internal differences and
transferable directly to external ones:

| Frame | Attested |
|---|---|
| `This difference can be explained by ...` | ×2 |
| `This discrepancy arises from ...` | ×2 |
| `This discrepancy is primarily due to ...` | ×1 |
| `... is attributed to ...` | ×4 |
| `... a difference primarily attributable to ...` | ×1 |
| `The reason is that ...` | ×2 |

`we attribute` is 0. `at odds with` is 0. `disagree` is 0. `contrary to` (2) is reserved for
contrasting the paper's own scenarios. **The author never appears as the subject of the
disagreement.** The difference is the subject and it is explained by a mechanism.

**The conversion.** A draft that says

> Our estimate is much higher than [12], who we believe underestimate distribution costs by
> assuming an implausibly high utilisation.

should say

> [12] report a lower delivered cost of X €/MWh. The difference is primarily attributable to
> their assumption of Y per cent network utilisation, against Z per cent here.

**Where the corpus runs out.** No published article shows the argument that survived peer
review, only its outcome, so there is no attested move for defending a contested result
against a referee who has already rejected the explanation. `revision.md` covers what can be
said about that from outside the corpus, and labels its sources.
