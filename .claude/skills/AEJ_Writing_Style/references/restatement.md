# Restatement: one number, four places

A headline figure appears in the highlights, the abstract, the results, the discussion and
the conclusion. Five appearances of the same quantity, and a draft that tries to avoid
looking repetitive by varying the wording ends up varying the number, the unit or the
condition, which is worse.

I traced a single headline number through six corpus papers, appearance by appearance. The
rule that comes out is short.

> **The number, its unit, its case labels and its qualifying condition are held constant,
> word for word. What changes is the clause wrapped around them.**

Repetition of the quantity is not a fault in this journal. It is the design. A reader who
opens at the conclusion is meant to get every headline figure without turning back.

## The traces

### AE5, `$0.70/kg`, four appearances

**Abstract**

> (1) For a storage capacity of 10⁷ kg and one injection–withdrawal cycle per year, the
> LCOHS is $0.70/kg for SC, $0.76/kg for DGR, and $0.92/kg for LRC. These values are
> significantly more advantageous compared to surface storage.

**Results**

> The final LCOHS for different scenarios are shown with the lowest cost for SC at $0.70/kg,
> DGR at $0.76/kg, and the highest for LRC at $0.92/kg. These results reflect the relative
> economics for a hydrogen storage project.

**Conclusion**

> At a 10⁷ kg storage scale with one injection–withdrawal cycle per year, the LCOHS is
> significantly lower for geological storage: $0.70/kg in SC, $0.76/kg in DGR, and $0.92/kg
> in LRC.

Constant across all three: the three values, the three case labels, their order, the
currency, the unit, and the condition (`10⁷ kg`, `one injection–withdrawal cycle per year`).
Changed: the abstract states, the results locate the values in the presentation (*are
shown*) and rank them (*the lowest / the highest*), the conclusion re-states the condition
and adds the comparative judgement (*significantly lower for geological storage*).

### AE9, `5.6 %`, three appearances

**Abstract**

> The results demonstrate that the heat storage proportion of the conventional steam-heated
> molten salt system **is only 5.6 %**, requiring 12 h of charging to supply 4.7 h of steam.
> In contrast, the molten salt coupled steam accumulator system ...

**Results**

> The heat storage proportion of the MS system **is only 5.6 %**, which is much lower than
> that of the 63.7 % of the MS-SA system, indicating that the heat storage ability of the
> conventional system is insufficient.

**Conclusion**

> (2) The heat storage proportion and overall energy efficiency of the molten salt system
> **are only 5.6 %** and 7.1 %, while the molten salt coupled steam accumulator system can
> achieve 63.7 % and 63.5 %, respectively.

The word `only` survives verbatim in all three. So does the contrast partner, `63.7 %`. The
abstract adds the operational consequence (12 h of charging for 4.7 h of steam), the results
add the interpretation (*indicating that ... is insufficient*), the conclusion bundles a
second metric alongside and numbers the point.

### AE11, `12.7` / `12.73 Mton`, four appearances

**Highlight**

> rLPG could substitute up to 12.7 Mton of fossil fuels in Chile by 2050.

**Abstract**

> Results indicate that rLPG could substitute up to 12.7 Mton of conventional fossil fuels,
> depending on the stringency of transition pathways.

**Results**

> By the year 2050, the potential demand for rLPG is estimated to reach 12.73 Mton,
> representing a 21.20% increase over the carbon neutral scenario (10.11 Mton) and a 28.40%
> rise compared to ...

**Conclusion**

> The results indicate that rLPG could substitute between 9.09 and 12.73 Mton of conventional
> fossil fuels by 2050, depending on the stringency of the transition pathway.

Two moves here that the other traces do not show.

**Precision increases with depth.** Highlight and abstract carry `12.7`; results and
conclusion carry `12.73`. Rounding for the front matter is expected, not sloppy, provided
the rounding is consistent and the full figure appears where the calculation is described.

**The conclusion widens to the range where the abstract gave only the bound.** The abstract
says *up to 12.7*; the conclusion says *between 9.09 and 12.73*. The abstract sells the
headline, the conclusion delivers the interval. Both use *depending on the stringency of the
transition pathway*, which is the same qualifying clause reused nearly verbatim.

And note how close the abstract and conclusion sentences are to each other. *Results indicate
that rLPG could substitute ... of conventional fossil fuels ... depending on the stringency
of transition pathways* against *The results indicate that rLPG could substitute ... of
conventional fossil fuels by 2050, depending on the stringency of the transition pathway.*
Near-verbatim reuse of the abstract's sentence in the conclusion is normal here and passes
review.

### AE10, `52.70 %`

**Abstract**

> For the hybrid way of A2P with B2P integration, the overall average efficiency and exergy
> efficiency stand at 52.70 % and 44.30 %, respectively.

**Results**

> ... energy efficiency of 52.70 %. This is more than 36.18 % higher than the 38.70 %
> efficiency of the methanol-based hydrogen storage and hydrogenation route.

**Discussion**

> S4 achieves an energy efficiency of 52.70 %, which is lower than the 88–89 % efficiency of
> ABS [79] but addresses the limitation of ABS in storage duration.

The discussion is where the **external** benchmark enters. Abstract and results compare
inside the paper; the discussion is the first place a competitor technology from the
literature is set against the number.

### AE1, `102,585.75 KT`, and AE8, `9.9 years`

Both hold the digits fixed across abstract, results and conclusion. AE8 shows the paired
form: *shortens the payback period from 9.9 to 7.4 years* (abstract), *the payback period is
shortened from 9.9 years to 7.4 years, significantly enhancing the project's attractiveness*
(results). The before-and-after pair travels together as a unit and is never split.

## The pattern, as a rule set

| Position | Precision | What the clause adds | Attested verb |
|---|---|---|---|
| Highlight | rounded, 1 dp | the actor and the horizon, no condition | `could substitute` |
| Abstract | rounded, 1 dp | the condition under which it holds | `stand at`, `is only`, `indicate that` |
| Results | full | where it is shown, and the internal contrast partner | `is estimated to reach`, `are shown with` |
| Discussion | full | the external benchmark from the literature | `which is lower than ... [79]` |
| Conclusion | full | the range, and the ranking or judgement, numbered | `is significantly lower for`, `are only` |

## What must not vary

- **The digits.** Round once, at the front matter, and never re-round differently.
- **The unit and currency.**
- **The case or scenario labels, and their order.** SC before DGR before LRC every time.
- **The qualifying condition.** If the abstract says *at one cycle per year*, the conclusion
  says it too. Dropping the condition on restatement is the most common way a correct number
  becomes a wrong claim.
- **`only`, and any other evaluative word attached to the number.** It is part of the
  finding.

## What must vary

Only the frame. If the abstract already said *significantly lower*, the results should say
where the value is shown and what it is lower *than*, and the conclusion should rank it. Three
sentences that all say *significantly lower* with no added work is the actual repetition
problem, and it is a problem of the clause, never of the digits.

## Checking it

Before submitting, pick each headline number and grep the manuscript for it. You should find
it in the abstract, in the section where it is computed, and in the conclusion, with
identical digits and an identical qualifying condition each time. A number that appears once
is under-sold. A number whose condition changes between appearances is a defect, and it is
the defect a careful referee finds first.
