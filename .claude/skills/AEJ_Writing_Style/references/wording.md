# Wording and idea presentation

The sentence-level craft: what goes in the subject slot, how one sentence hooks to the
next, which verb carries which strength of claim, and how a single finding is unpacked
into a paragraph. Drawn from 4,788 body sentences across the 14-paper corpus.

`anatomy.md` covers what each section contains. `phrasebank.md` gives frames by function.
This document covers how the sentences themselves are built.

## 1. The subject slot

**The grammatical subject is almost always a thing, not a person.** Across 4,788 body
sentences, *We* opens 22 of them, about one in every 220. The subject is the model, the
cost, the results, the figure, the approach, the technology.

> **The cost of** the cushion gas depends on the amount and price of hydrogen.
> **The PV capacity factors** are lower than commonly reported global values.
> **The extremely low gravimetric density of gaseous H2 (GH2)** poses significant
> technical and economic challenges for bulk export.
> **Fig. 6 presents** the simulated temperature fields.
> **This approach** facilitates a scientific evaluation of the economic feasibility.

*We* is reserved for genuine authorial acts, and then it is used without apology: *we
propose*, *we develop*, *we find*, *we consider*, *we incorporated*. It is not used for
navigation (*we now turn to*) or for opinion (*we believe*).

The practical consequence: when a draft sentence begins with an authorial gesture, ask
what thing the sentence is really about and promote that to subject.

## 2. The cohesion engine: demonstrative chaining

**Nine per cent of sentences open with *This* or *These*.** This is how the corpus binds
prose together, and it is the most transferable single habit after the trailing
participle.

Two families.

**(a) *This* + reporting verb**, which interprets the previous sentence.

> **This indicates that** negative correlation implies benefits from an LCOE perspective.
> **This suggests that** …
> **This highlights the** value as an efficiency measure.
> **This demonstrates** …
> **This implies** …
> **This means** …
> **This underscores** …
> **This enables** …
> **This allows us to assess** the alignment between the cost-optimal strategy and current
> political objectives.
> **This is because** …
> **This is primarily due to** the assumption of horizontal panel orientation.

**(b) *This* + a summary noun that labels what the previous sentence said.** This is the
more sophisticated move, because the noun both links and classifies.

Nouns seen in that slot: *approach, study, finding, results, values, discrepancy, trend,
pattern, behaviour, choice, method, gap, effect, difference, equilibrium, constraint,
challenge, limitation, aggregation, distinction*.

> The PV capacity factors are lower than commonly reported global values. **This
> discrepancy** is primarily due to the assumption of horizontal panel orientation.

> …the parties arrived at an agreement marking the "beginning of the end" of the fossil
> fuel era. **This global act of recognition** underscores the imperative need for a
> transition away from fossil fuels.

> A thermal accumulation effect emerges over long-term operation. **This equilibrium** is
> sustained by continuous solar input.

Naming the previous sentence with a noun is worth practising deliberately. It forces you
to decide what the previous sentence *was*, whether a discrepancy, a trend, a choice or a
limitation, and the reader gets that classification free.

## 3. Given before new

Each sentence starts with material the reader already has and ends with what is new. The
new thing then becomes the given thing at the start of the next sentence. That chain is
why the prose reads as flat and inevitable.

> Chemically synthesised ammonia (NH3) **has a long history of use as a fertiliser**,
> leading to **the development of infrastructures for its production, storage, and
> transportation at export scale**. MeOH **is another attractive option** due to **its
> relatively high volumetric H2 density and gravimetric H2 content**. **Its liquid state
> under ambient conditions** eliminates **the need for additional transformation costs**.

Each sentence's opening noun phrase was already in play. Nothing arrives cold.

The failure mode this prevents: a paragraph in which every sentence opens on a new noun,
which reads as a list of assertions rather than an argument.

## 4. Reporting verbs, from weakest to strongest

The corpus keeps a clear ladder, and picks a rung deliberately. Counts across the corpus:

| Verb | Uses | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| *may* | 65 | possibility |
| *might* | 7 | possibility, rarer |
| *could* | 85 | conditional possibility |
| *suggest(s)* | 35 | weak evidence |
| *likely* | 24 | probable |
| *can* | 375 | capability, not evidence |
| *indicate(s)* | 78 | the default for a result |
| *show(s)* | 126 | the default for a result |
| *reveal(s)* | — | a result that was hidden |
| *demonstrate(s)* | 34 | strong, used sparingly |
| *confirm(s)* | 12 | agreement with prior work |
| *prove(s)* | 6 | almost never |

**The working default is *show* or *indicate*.** *Demonstrate* is reserved for a result
the paper considers settled. *Confirm* is used when the finding agrees with something
already published, not for the paper's own novel claim. *Prove* is effectively absent, and
a draft that reaches for it will read as overclaiming.

*Can* is the most frequent modal but is not a hedge: it states capability. *The system can
store part of the sensible heat* is a fact about the design, not a hedged result.

## 5. Causation

**The prepositional form dominates.** *Due to* appears 149 times; *because of* only 11.
The corpus attaches a cause as a phrase rather than opening a subordinate clause.

| Form | Uses | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| *due to* | 149 | the workhorse |
| *result(s) in* | 34 | forward causation |
| *thereby* | 28 | consequence in the same sentence |
| *lead(s) to* | 24 | forward causation, often as a participle |
| *driven by* | 23 | attaching a driver to a number |
| *as a result* | 18 | sentence opener |
| *attributed to* | 14 | assigning a share of a total |
| *because of* | 11 | rare |
| *owing to* | 4 | formal, usually in an abstract |
| *stem(s) from* | 4 | rare |

> MeOH is another attractive option **due to** its relatively high volumetric H2 density.
> …a 2-fold LCOA differential ($601–$1203/ton) **driven by** solar-wind resource quality.
> …with 34% **attributed to** PV CAPEX.
> …combining energy sources may reduce required transmission capacity, **thereby**
> reducing cost.

*Driven by* and *attributed to* are worth having in hand specifically for numbers: they
let a figure carry its explanation without a second sentence.

## 6. Comparison

| Form | Uses |
|---|---|
| *respectively* | 159 |
| *compared to / compared with* | 105 |
| *than the / than those* | 59 |
| *in contrast* | 39 |
| *whereas* | 35 |
| *relative to* | 25 |
| *N times higher / lower* | 3 |
| *versus* | 3 |

**`respectively` is the most used comparison device in the corpus.** It lets one sentence
carry parallel results that would otherwise need three:

> The LCOH for the LH2, LNH3 and MCH pathways was 7.9, 5.4 and 5.6 USD/kg, respectively.
> …thermal power generation and installed capacity accounting for 69.95 % and 48 % in
> 2023, respectively.

**`whereas` carries the contrast inside a sentence; `In contrast,` opens the next one.**
Both are neutral and neither is on anybody's banned list.

*Versus* is almost absent in prose, though it is common in figure axis labels. Prefer
*compared with*.

## 7. Magnitude, approximation, and the word *only*

| Word | Uses |
|---|---|
| *only* | 153 |
| *over* | 103 |
| *approximately* | 76 |
| *about* | 43 |
| *up to* | 40 |
| *less than* | 22 |
| *roughly* | 16 |
| *nearly* | 16 |
| *more than* | 10 |

**`only` is the corpus's most used quantifier, and it is doing rhetorical work.** It marks
a number as smaller than the reader might expect, which is how a paper signals
significance without editorialising:

> Globally, the cumulative installations of wave power reached **only** 30 MW by 2024.
> It estimates that freshwater withdrawal for global hydrogen production will account for
> **only** 2.4%.
> **only** green hydrogen plants connected directly to run-of-river hydropower plants are
> currently profitable.

**`approximately` is the default hedge on a figure**, at nearly five times the rate of
*roughly*. A draft that prefers *roughly* and *about* is not wrong but is off-register.

### The intensifier is standardised, not avoided

General writing advice says to strip intensifiers. This journal does the opposite: it
picks one and uses it constantly.

| Word | Uses |
|---|---|
| `significantly` | **85** |
| `notably` | 28 |
| `substantially` | 14 |
| `greatly` | 6 |
| `dramatically` | 3 |
| `considerably` | 3 |
| `markedly` | 3 |

So the rule is not *avoid intensifiers*. It is **use `significantly`, not its synonyms**,
and only where the number that justifies it is in the same sentence:

> The OPEX of S3 (38.22 MUSD) is **significantly** lower than that of S4 (49.04 MUSD),
> primarily due to revenue from ...
> Compared to S1, the capacity of the hydrogen storage tank in S3 is **significantly**
> reduced.
> These values are **significantly** more advantageous compared to surface storage.

`significantly` unattached to a figure is the only version worth cutting, and *dramatically*
should be converted to `significantly` rather than deleted. Note that the corpus uses
`significantly` in its plain-English sense of *by a large margin*. Where a paper also
reports statistical tests, the word carries a second meaning, and the two need keeping
apart: *statistically significant* appears in the corpus as its own fixed phrase.

## 8. Nominalisation and the *of*-chain

The corpus turns processes into nouns and hangs *of*-phrases from them. Commonest heads:
*the development of, the importance of, the influence of, the production of, the
integration of, the availability of, the profitability of, the distribution of, the
absence of, the feasibility of, the deployment of, the utilization of, the contribution
of, the sensitivity of, the duration of*.

> To ascertain **the contribution of** each component to the landed cost **of** GH2 at the
> import port in Japan, Fig. 11 depicts a breakdown **of** final LCOH.

> …**the absence of** benchmark valuation criteria also makes it challenging to compare
> natural hydrogen projects with other hydrogen production pathways.

> …**the integration of** solar collectors significantly enhances the thermal and economic
> performance **of** the system.

Plain-English style guides discourage this; the journal does it constantly, and a draft
written entirely in verbs will read as informal here. Two or three *of*-links in a chain
is normal. Four or more becomes hard to parse even by this standard.

## 9. Where the number goes

Three placements, all common, chosen by what the sentence is emphasising.

**Number as the predicate**, when the value is the point:

> Under S1 and base case assumptions, the landed LCOH would be 11.3 AUD/kg.

**Number in parentheses**, when the claim is the point and the value is support:

> …a 2-fold Levelized Cost of Ammonia (LCOA) differential ($601–$1203/ton) driven by
> solar-wind resource quality.
> Identified optimal drilling depth (1375–1560 m) and flow rate (6.7–9.5 kg/s).

**Number with its decomposition trailing**, when the breakdown matters:

> …at 95.7 % (15.5 % for drilling, 65.8 % for mining, and 14.4 % for lining).
> …with 34% attributed to PV CAPEX, followed by 16.7% and 13.6% from LNH3 storage units
> and PEM, respectively.

The same quantity is often restated in a second unit so the reader never converts:

> 15,000 m3/day (approximately 56 kg/hour or 1.3 tons/day)
> less than 6 tons/day (250 kg/hour)

## 10. Tense

Three conventions, applied consistently.

**Present for what the paper and the model do**, and for general truths:

> The model **aims** to determine the ideal geographic distribution.
> Hydrogen **holds** a prominent position among energy carriers.
> This study **presents** a global assessment.

**Past for what was done to produce the data**, and for what a specific prior study found:

> The base year **was selected** as 2030.
> Electricity **was generated** by photovoltaic arrays.
> They **found** that the capacity factor of a PtG system drives the economic case.

**Present for what the results say**, even though the run is finished:

> The results **indicate** that rLPG could substitute between 9.09 and 12.73 Mton.
> Fig. 5 **presents** the average capacity factors.

Mixing past and present inside one paragraph is normal and correct when the jobs differ:
*prices **were taken** from Eurostat* sits happily beside *the model **resolves** demand
to NUTS3*.

## 11. Unpacking one finding into a paragraph

The corpus has a repeatable five-step expansion. Use it when a finding deserves a
paragraph rather than a sentence.

1. **Claim**, stated flat, with the thing as subject.
2. **The numbers that establish it**, base case first.
3. **The key comparison**, flagged *Importantly* or *Notably*.
4. **Interpretation**, opening *This* plus a summary noun or a reporting verb.
5. **A one-line takeaway**, often opening *Hence* or *Therefore*.

Worked, from the corpus:

> **[1]** First, the study confirms that economies of scale are the most powerful lever
> for cost reduction. **[2]** The base scenario presents a small-scale production from
> field test results, which led to 15,000 m3/day (approximately 56 kg/hour or 1.3
> tons/day) across ten vertical wells, resulting in an LCOH of $6.82 per kilogram.
> **[3]** Importantly, scaling production capacity by a factor of six to 336 kg/hour (8
> tons/day) reduces the LCOH by more than 60%. **[4]** This result emphasizes the
> nonlinear nature of hydrogen cost formation, where capital-intensive components are
> rapidly diluted as throughput increases. **[5]** Hence achieving commercial scale is
> critical for competitiveness.

The same five steps carry a negative finding, a sensitivity result or a validation.

## 12. Sentence openers

What a body sentence actually starts with, by frequency:

*The* (839) · *This* (332) · *In* (281) · *For* (139) · *However,* (115) · *These* (98) ·
*A* (85) · *It* (79) · *To* (75) · *As* (72) · *Table* (71) · *Fig.* (63) · *While* (54) ·
*Therefore,* (53) · *Additionally,* (47) · *Furthermore,* (39) · *Although* (35) ·
*By* (34) · *According* (30) · *Moreover,* (28) · *When* (24) · *Notably,* (23) ·
*Consequently,* (22) · *We* (22) · *With* (21) · *Under* (21) · *Finally,* (20) ·
*Similarly,* (20) · *Since* (20) · *Given* (19) · *Conversely,* (18) · *Compared* (17)

Two things worth taking from that list. **134 sentences open with *Table* or *Fig.***,
which is the float-as-subject habit at scale. And **explicit connectives open roughly one
sentence in fourteen**, which is far denser than an essayistic draft, where the logical
relation is usually left implicit in the sentence's shape.

## 13. Parallelism and lists

Aims, contributions and findings are enumerated, either inline with (i), (ii), (iii) or as
a bulleted or numbered list.

> …to: (i) compare the levelized cost and levelized value of power-to-gas across
> locations; (ii) identify potential hotspots for their future implementation in
> Switzerland; and (iii) set cost improvement targets as well as smart deployment
> strategies.

> The main conclusions are as follows: (1) … (2) …

Inline lists keep strict parallelism of grammatical form, and the last item takes *and*:

> …including construction, operation, maintenance, and remediation expenses
> …such as export cables, substations, and shared maintenance logistics
> …maritime traffic routes, protected and ecologically sensitive areas, tourism and
> recreation zones, aquaculture-related areas, submarine cable corridors, and
> depth-constrained zones

## 14. Introducing something the reader may not know

A definition paragraph, in three moves: what it is, what it includes, what it is not.

> Co-located or hybrid offshore energy parks **refer to** integrated systems where two or
> more renewable technologies (e.g., wind, wave, floating solar) are deployed within a
> shared offshore area and connected to common infrastructure, such as export cables,
> substations, and shared maintenance logistics. **This contrasts with** traditional
> single-source parks that operate independently.

A metric is defined the same way, then anchored to a familiar one before its equation:

> The Levelized Cost of Hydrogen Storage (LCOHS) **is an economic concept used to
> evaluate** the economic benefits of different hydrogen storage technologies. LCOHS
> **encompasses** the entire life-cycle costs associated with a specific hydrogen storage
> technology, including construction, operation, maintenance, and remediation expenses,
> and **derives** an average cost per unit of hydrogen stored. **This approach
> facilitates** a scientific evaluation of the economic feasibility. The LCOHS **is
> similar to the concept of** Levelized Cost of Electricity (LCOE), which is commonly used
> in the energy industry, and its formula **can be derived from** the latter [59].

Definitions also appear inside the results when the analysis needs one:

> In the following analysis, "all locations" **refers to** the full set of feasible
> offshore sites (n = 22,713), **while** "co-located" or "mixed" **refers to** the subset
> of locations where the cost-optimal solution includes two or more technologies.

## 15. What the corpus never does

Useful as a negative checklist, because these are exactly the habits an essayistic draft
brings with it.

- **Opens on an aphorism or a general truth in the author's own voice.** The first
  sentence of every paper is a cited fact about the world.
- **Withholds the point.** No paragraph builds to a revelation; the claim is in sentence
  one.
- **Uses repetition for emphasis.** No *the outcome settles, and it settles on…*
- **Inverts for effect.** No *Decisive is the capital recovery test.*
- **Leaves a logical relation implicit.** If two clauses contrast, *whereas* or *however*
  is present.
- **Characterises prior work without numbers.** Every claim about a cited study carries
  that study's figure.
- **Uses a finding as a section heading.** Headings are noun phrases.
- **Says *prove*.** Six uses across 188,000 words, and none of them for the paper's own
  central claim.
- **Leaves a concession hanging.** Every *while grounded in…* or *despite this
  constraint…* is followed by a recovery in the same or the next sentence.

## 16. Sentence length, and why not to vary it

Measured over 5,221 body sentences:

| | Words |
|---|---|
| Median | **24** |
| Mean | 25.4 |
| p10 | 11 |
| p25 | 16 |
| p75 | 32 |
| p90 | 43 |

Twenty-eight per cent of sentences run past 30 words. Eleven per cent come in under 12.

The distribution is not the interesting part. The **ordering** is. A sentence over 30 words
is followed by another sentence over 30 words 504 times, and followed by a sentence under 12
words only 152 times. Long sentences cluster. There is no deliberate long-then-short
alternation anywhere in the corpus.

**So the standard advice to vary sentence length for rhythm is wrong for this journal.** A
short punchy sentence dropped after a long one reads as an essayist's move, because here it
is one. The house sound is uniform and medium-long: a claim, its qualifying condition, and
its citation, which lands at twenty to thirty words almost every time.

The short sentences that do occur are not rhetorical. They are structural, and they fall in
predictable places:

> The workflow is validated against field data.
> This difference is statistically significant.
> Neither pathway of H2P nor A2P achieves lifecycle profitability.
> This paper is organized as follows.

A methods declaration, a test outcome, a headline verdict, a roadmap announcement. If a short
sentence in a draft is not doing one of those four jobs, it is probably there for effect and
should be joined to its neighbour.

**Practical check.** If a paragraph's sentences run 34, 9, 28, 7 words, the two short ones are
almost certainly rhetorical. If they run 26, 31, 22, 29, the paragraph is in register even
though it looks monotonous on the page. Monotony is correct here.

## 17. The discourse-marker budget

Markers are not interchangeable, and the corpus's proportions are lopsided enough that using
them evenly marks a draft as foreign.

| Contrastive | Uses | | Additive and sequencing | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `However,` | **135** | | `Additionally,` | **52** |
| `While` | 53 | | `Furthermore,` | 43 |
| `Although` | 40 | | `Moreover,` | 33 |
| `In contrast,` | 28 | | `Finally,` | 24 |
| `Conversely,` | 18 | | `Notably,` | 24 |
| `Nevertheless,` | 15 | | `In addition,` | 19 |
| `Despite` | 15 | | `Overall,` | 15 |
| `Nonetheless,` | 8 | | `First,` / `Second,` / `Third,` | 14 / 10 / 5 |
| `On the other hand` | 6 | | `Specifically,` | 11 |
| `Yet` | 1 | | `Importantly,` | **3** |

Four things follow.

**`However,` carries the contrastive load almost single-handedly**, at more than twice the
next marker. A draft that spreads its qualifications across *nevertheless*, *nonetheless*,
*conversely* and *on the other hand* to avoid repeating *however* is optimising for something
this journal does not value. Repeat *however*.

**`Additionally,` outranks `Moreover,`**, which inverts the usual assumption. `Moreover` is
the more literary of the two and is correspondingly rarer here.

**`Importantly,` is almost absent** at three uses, and `Interestingly,` does not appear at
all. Both tell the reader how to feel about a finding, which is the essayist's job rather
than the reporter's. If a result matters, its number says so.

**`Yet` as a sentence opener is effectively unused**, one instance in 188,000 words, and
`On the other hand` is rare at six. Both belong to a more conversational register.

The numbered sequence `First, / Second, / Third,` is used, but sparingly and only where three
or more parallel items genuinely follow. Note the taper, 14 to 10 to 5: some sequences start
and are then folded into other structures, usually the bracketed `(1) (2) (3)` list that
`stance.md` §4 describes for conclusions.
