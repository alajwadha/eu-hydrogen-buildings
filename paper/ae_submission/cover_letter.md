To the Editors, *Applied Energy*

Dear Editors,

We are pleased to submit our manuscript, **"Hydrogen in residential heating and the power system: a merit-order and capital-recovery assessment for 29 European markets,"** for consideration as a research article in *Applied Energy*.

Europe must replace hundreds of billions of euros of residential heating plant by 2050, and the choice between electrifying heat with heat pumps and reusing the gas network for hydrogen shapes how that money is spent. Continental cost-optimisation models resolve the building stock in detail but at national spatial resolution, so they see neither the within-country demand gradient nor the storage geology that decides where hydrogen competes; assessments at building-stock resolution instead fix hydrogen's share exogenously. Our contribution is to impose hydrogen's adoption **exogenously** in four transparent scenarios and then test it against a dispatch-supported potential derived independently, which neither sets nor caps adoption, pricing hydrogen as a system operator would across three linked arenas, the building winter peak, district heating, and the power sector, on a demand surface resolved to NUTS3 and aggregated to the country at which every cost and dispatch test is run, across the EU-27, the United Kingdom and Switzerland.

The analysis reframes the hydrogen-for-heat question from an energy-share problem into one of firm capacity and capital recovery, and reports several decision-relevant findings:

- On the full annual cost of delivered heat in 2050, the best heat pump undercuts the hydrogen boiler in 22 of 29 countries (medians €118.5 against €122.8/MWh, and €132.7/MWh for a gas boiler), at a median gap of €15.4/MWh. Hydrogen holds the remaining seven, five of them salt-cavern markets, because heating draws about three-quarters of its energy in winter while electrolytic production is far flatter, so the molecule must be stored between seasons and geology sets what that costs.
- Priced on short-run marginal cost, hydrogen wins the winter power peak only in seven salt-cavern markets. That count is invariant to the scarcity premium by construction, since the premium cancels from the comparison, and it is stable across a storage-adder band of roughly €50 to 75/MWh-heat.
- No winning hydrogen peaker recovers its capital from market rent (about 9% capacity-weighted by 2050, spanning 5 to 40% across the five that build a peaker as run windows range from 48 to 343 hours); a standing capacity payment of roughly €31 to 63/kW-yr would be required.
- That payment attaches to low-utilisation firm capacity rather than to hydrogen specifically, and the point sharpens further, since a gas peaker needs a *smaller* absolute payment in the same markets (€45 vs €51/kW-yr, capacity-weighted across the five that build a peaker in 2050; gas is cheaper in four of those five, covering 96% of winning capacity), so the modelled technology-neutral comparison procures unabated gas, a result that turns on the capex gap and reverses below about €520 to 540/kW. Selecting hydrogen therefore requires a condition on emissions. We report the implied cost of that condition in the manuscript, and we flag there that it is computed on a price series the hydrogen unit itself can set, so it is an indicative procurement premium rather than a clean entry counterfactual. Europe's existing condition, Article 22(4) of Regulation (EU) 2019/943, does not bind an open-cycle gas peaker on either limb.
- The 2050 residential-heating emissions gap is set by the fossil-phaseout lever. We report this as a property of the model's construction rather than as evidence of robustness.

One methodological point deserves explicit disclosure at submission rather than discovery in review. The base-load result agrees in direction with the review literature the manuscript cites, but it is reached on a different accounting, and that accounting decides the count. We size equipment capital to peak heat demand and annualise it over the heat that capacity delivers, we charge each carrier its own last-mile network, and we charge hydrogen the seasonal store its winter-weighted load requires. Applying the first two without the third puts hydrogen ahead in 17 of 29 markets; applying all three returns 22 of 29 to the heat pump. The Supplementary Information reports that sequence in full, because the intermediate result is what a reader would obtain from the more common accounting, and because it shows how much of the comparison turns on charging one molecule the same way in every test.

We are equally explicit about what the result does not establish. Two assumptions still run in hydrogen's favour. The comparison sets an untaxed molecule at roughly €50/MWh delivered against taxed retail electricity at roughly €209/MWh, and it prices the molecule green where a detailed assessment of the German heating case argues the molecule would in practice be blue, which our own route costing puts at roughly €81/MWh. Both would narrow or close the seven-market exception rather than widen it, so the heat-pump result should be read as conservative. The dispatch, capacity and capital-recovery findings do not depend on either assumption. They do depend on a third, which the manuscript calls the strongest assumption in the paper. The power layer runs on one realisation of a single synthetic weather year, with a six-day wind lull imposed simultaneously on all 29 countries. Every power-sector number in the manuscript is conditional on that construction, the firm-fleet requirement carries a band of about ±9% from the assumed event length alone, and we present none of it as an adequacy assessment.

Four headline numbers are benchmarked against published values rather than left to stand on the model alone: the demand reconstruction against the independent Hotmaps regional baseline, the base-load ordering against a European household comparison built on a different accounting and against the review literature, the seasonal-storage rates against the bulk-storage literature, and the €31 to 63/kW-yr capacity payment against the £60/kW-yr the 2024 British four-year-ahead auction cleared at, roughly €70/kW-yr at 2024 rates. A clearing price and a modelled revenue requirement are different objects, so that last comparison is a check on magnitude and not a validation; what it does corroborate directly is the technology-neutrality corollary, since the same auction procured 27.3 GW of unabated gas out of 43.1 GW.

We believe the work fits *Applied Energy*'s scope. It is a rigorous, reproducible, policy-relevant energy-systems assessment with direct implications for buildings decarbonisation and power-system adequacy. The full model, input-data provenance and figure generators are openly available under an MIT licence, and a Supplementary Information document provides the complete methodology, parameter tables, per-country results and sensitivity analyses.
